Who owns your domain name is a question most business owners never ask until something goes wrong. The web developer stops answering, the site goes down, or an invoice turns up for a renewal you do not remember. Then it turns out the business name on the record is not yours, or the only login belongs to someone who left years ago.

The short answer: look your domain up in the [.au WHOIS tool](https://www.auda.org.au/au-domain-names/domain-name-help/using-whois) run by auDA, the body that manages .au names. The Registrant field should show your business's legal name, and the Registrant ID should be your own ABN or ACN. If it shows your developer, a marketing agency or a business that no longer exists, fix it now while everyone is still talking to each other.

I set up domains for clients and move them between providers when a site is rebuilt. The domain is the one piece that everything else hangs off. Your website, your email and every link Google has to your business all depend on it.

## Who owns your domain name: what the WHOIS record shows

Strictly, nobody owns a .au domain. You hold a licence to use it for a set period, and auDA's rules call the licence holder the registrant. The WHOIS record for a .com.au shows:

- **Registrant.** The legal name of the person or entity that holds the licence. This is the line that matters.
- **Registrant ID.** Usually an ABN or ACN, which is how the name proved it was eligible for .com.au.
- **Registrar.** The accredited company the name is registered through, with its website.
- **Reseller.** Filled in only if the name was bought through a reseller, such as a hosting company or a web agency.
- **Status.** Whether the name is active, on hold or expired.

The registrar and reseller tell you where the account lives. auDA explains that you can [find your provider](https://auda.org.au/au-domain-names/domain-name-help/how-find-your-registrar) from those fields. That is the company you contact to log in, renew or move the name.

## What it means if your web developer is listed

Plenty of developers register domains for clients, and that is fine if they do it properly. auDA's [licensing rules](https://files.auda.org.au/documents/au-Licensing-Rules-July-2026.pdf) allow an agent to apply for a name on someone's behalf, but say the agent must make sure the person they are acting for is recorded as the registrant (clause 2.2.6). So a developer can buy the name for you and keep it in their account, but your business should still be on the record.

There are really three situations:

| What the WHOIS shows | What it means | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Your business is the registrant, the developer is the reseller | Normal. The name is yours, they manage the account | Ask for your own login, or the authorisation code, so you are not dependent on them |
| The developer's business is the registrant | The licence is legally theirs, even if you paid for it | Ask them to transfer it to you while the relationship is good |
| A business that no longer trades is the registrant | A risk. Renewals and transfers get hard | Contact the registrar and ask what they need to sort it out |

If you need a transfer of registrant, the [licensing rules](https://files.auda.org.au/documents/au-Licensing-Rules-July-2026.pdf) set the conditions out in clause 2.13. The current registrant asks the registrar in writing, the new holder has to meet the eligibility rules, and the licence must not have expired. The new holder takes out a fresh licence and pays for it. Most registrars have a form for this. It is not difficult, but it needs the current registrant to cooperate, which is why it is worth doing before there is a falling-out.

## Getting into the account

Being the registrant and being able to log in are different things. The person who registered the name may have used their own email, or the account may sit inside an agency's reseller account with dozens of other clients.

The useful piece is the authorisation code. It is the password that lets you move a domain to a different provider. Under the [licensing rules](https://files.auda.org.au/documents/au-Licensing-Rules-July-2026.pdf), the registrar has to give it to the registrant contact on the record when the registrant asks in writing (clause 2.12.10). The same rules say a registrant can also retrieve the code, and check the creation or expiry date, through auDA's registry recovery tool (clause 2.12.11). That only works if the registrant contact email on the record is one you can still read.

So, while you are checking:

1. Note the registrar and reseller from the WHOIS.
2. Find out which email address is the registrant contact. Your provider can tell you.
3. Change it to an address the business controls long term, such as an info@ or admin@ address, not a staff member's personal inbox.
4. Write down where the account is, who can log in and when the name expires. Keep it with your other important records.

## What happens if the domain expires

A .au licence runs for one to five years. auDA's page on [renewing a .au name](https://www.auda.org.au/au-domain-names/manage-your-au-domain-name/renew-your-au-domain-name/) sets out the timing:

- You can renew up to 90 days before the expiry date.
- After expiry there is a 30 day grace period to renew. Some providers charge an extra fee in that window.
- During the grace period your website and email stop working.
- At the end of the 30 days the name is removed and anyone can register it.

The reminders go to the contact details on the account. If those belong to someone who left, nobody sees them, and the first sign of trouble is the email going quiet. It is an easy way for a small business to lose a name it has used for years. For a business that relies on enquiries through the website, even a few days without email is a real cost.

Even a simple static site still has a domain to renew. Keeping an eye on that date is part of what I do under [website maintenance](/services/website-maintenance.html).

## Domain renewal scams: how to spot a fake invoice

The other reason to know your provider is that fake renewal notices are common, and they look convincing. The usual trick is a letter or email that reads like a renewal but is really selling you a new, similar name, such as the .com version of your .com.au. Scamwatch [describes the pattern](https://www.scamwatch.gov.au/node/446): an unsolicited notice that looks like an invoice, often with payment logos or a tear-off slip.

This is not a small problem. In 2018 the Federal Court ordered two companies to pay [$1.95 million in penalties](https://www.accc.gov.au/media-release/domain-name-corp-and-domain-name-agency-to-pay-195-million-in-penalties) after they sent about 300,000 notices that looked like renewals. Businesses paid them roughly $2.3 million. It has not stopped. Melbourne IT's [known scams page](https://support.melbourneit.au/hosting/known-scams) logged a new one in January 2026, noting that these messages look real because they use public details like the expiry date.

Before you pay anything about a domain, check:

- **The name matches exactly.** Consumer Affairs Victoria's advice is to [confirm the domain on the invoice](https://www.consumer.vic.gov.au/consumers-and-businesses/scams/small-business-scams/domain-name-scam) is yours, letter for letter, including the ending.
- **It comes from your provider.** The registrar or reseller in your WHOIS record, not a company you have never dealt with.
- **It is actually due.** Your provider's account shows the real expiry date.
- **You contact them yourself.** Use the login or phone number you already have, not a link or number in the message.

If one person in the business pays invoices, show them what a genuine renewal from your provider looks like.

## A ten-minute check

1. Look your domain up in the [.au WHOIS](https://www.auda.org.au/au-domain-names/domain-name-help/using-whois).
2. Confirm the registrant is your business and the ID is your ABN or ACN.
3. Note the registrar, the reseller and the expiry date.
4. Make sure you, not only your developer, can log in.
5. Set the contact email to an address the business will always have.
6. Turn on auto-renew, or put the expiry date in the calendar 90 days early.
